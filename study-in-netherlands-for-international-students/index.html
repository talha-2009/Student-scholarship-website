Study in Netherlands for International Students: Complete 2026 Guide to Universities, Scholarships, Visa & Costs

Last updated August 1, 2026 · 28 min read

Study in Netherlands for International Students has become one of the most popular routes into European higher education, and this guide explains exactly how to do it. More than 120,000 international students already study in the Netherlands, drawn by more than 2,100 English-taught degree programmes, several of the world's top-ranked universities, and a famously welcoming, highly educated society that ranks among the most English-fluent in Europe. If you are considering a bachelor's, master's, or PhD, this guide is for you: it walks through every step, from choosing a university and finding scholarships to meeting the requirements, budgeting, applying for a Netherlands student visa, and starting your career afterwards.

By the end of this guide you will know which Dutch universities fit your goals, what tuition fees and living costs to expect, which scholarships in the Netherlands you are eligible for, whether you can study without IELTS, how the visa and IND process works, and how to avoid the mistakes that delay thousands of applications every year.

Quick summary

  • Universities: 13 research universities; UvA, TU Delft, Utrecht, and Leiden lead global rankings.
  • Language: 2,100+ English-taught programmes; IELTS 6.0-7.0 typical, alternatives accepted.
  • Tuition (non-EU): roughly €8,000-€15,000/year bachelor's, €10,000-€20,000/year master's.
  • Living costs: about €1,000-€1,500/month; IND requires proof of ~€1,175/month.
  • Visa: university applies via the IND for an MVV + residence permit, usually 2-4 weeks.
  • Work: 16 hours/week during term, full-time in summer; 1-year post-study orientation visa.

Why Study in the Netherlands?

Students choose the Netherlands for a simple reason: it offers a top-ranked, English-friendly higher education system at a lower cost than many comparable destinations. Dutch universities combine academic excellence with a practical, problem-solving teaching style, and graduates are highly regarded by employers across Europe.

  • World-class universities at a reasonable price: TU Delft, the University of Amsterdam (UvA), Utrecht University, Leiden University, and Erasmus University Rotterdam consistently appear in the global top 150 of the QS World University Rankings, while charging international tuition that is often half the cost of equivalent UK or US programmes.
  • English-taught education: the Netherlands offers more than 2,100 English-taught study programmes and around 2,200 English-taught courses, the highest number of English-taught degrees in mainland Europe outside the UK. Over 95% of Dutch people speak English, so you do not need Dutch for daily life.
  • Innovative, interactive teaching: the Dutch education model emphasises problem-based learning, teamwork, presentations, and critical thinking rather than rote memorisation. This prepares graduates for modern, collaborative workplaces.
  • Career opportunities: the Netherlands hosts the European headquarters of major multinationals, including ASML, Shell, Philips, Unilever, and ING, and offers a one-year post-study orientation visa, making it easier to stay and find work after graduation.
  • Central location in Europe: Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and Utrecht are within a few hours of Paris, Berlin, London, and Brussels by train, so weekend travel across Europe is simple and affordable.
  • High quality of life and safety: Dutch cities are consistently ranked among the world's most livable, with excellent cycling infrastructure, reliable public transport, and a welcoming, multicultural society.

Who is this guide for? It is written for international students from Asia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, including students from Pakistan planning to study in the Netherlands, recent graduates looking for master's programmes, researchers seeking PhD positions, and parents researching safe, well-priced study destinations for their children.

Scholarships in the Netherlands for International Students

There are many scholarships in the Netherlands for international students, ranging from one-off grants to fully funded packages that cover tuition, living costs, travel, and insurance. Funding comes from the Dutch government, the European Union, universities, and private foundations, so your best strategy is to apply for university admission and scholarships at the same time.

Government and national scholarships in the Netherlands

  • Holland Scholarship: a one-off grant of €5,000 for the first year of study, offered by the Dutch Ministry of Education and participating universities to bachelor's and master's students from outside the EEA. Each university sets its own deadline and selection process. Check the official Holland Scholarship website and the Study in Holland listing for the current list of participating universities.
  • Orange Knowledge Programme (OKP): administered by Nuffic, this programme funds professionals from selected developing countries, including Pakistan, for master's degrees and short courses. Funding rounds have changed over time, so check the current Nuffic website for open calls and eligible countries.
  • Erasmus Mundus Joint Master's (EMJM): full-tuition scholarships plus a monthly stipend for students admitted to selected joint master's programmes offered by consortia of European universities. Scholarships are open to students worldwide. Search the official Erasmus Mundus catalogue.

Fully funded scholarships in the Netherlands

  • Erasmus Mundus Joint Master's scholarships cover full tuition and provide a monthly stipend for the full duration of the programme.
  • University full scholarships such as the Maastricht University High Potential Scholarship and the Radboud Scholarship Programme can cover tuition plus a monthly living allowance.
  • PhD positions are usually salaried employment contracts at Dutch research universities, which means a monthly salary, social security, and employee benefits rather than a traditional scholarship.

University scholarships in the Netherlands

  • Amsterdam Excellence Scholarship (UvA): €25,000 per year for outstanding non-EEA students in select programmes.
  • Leiden University Excellence Scholarship (LEXS): partial to nearly full tuition support for exceptional master's applicants. See the Leiden University Excellence Scholarship listing.
  • University of Twente Scholarship (UTS): a tuition and/or fee discount ranging from €3,000 to €22,000 per year.
  • University of Groningen offers several talent and excellence grants for international students.
  • TU Delft, TU/e, and Wageningen run excellence and faculty scholarships, including the TU Delft Global Excellence Scholarship and the WUR Excellence Programme.
  • VU Amsterdam Fellowship Programme and Erasmus University merit awards for non-EEA students.

Scholarships for Pakistani students: many Dutch scholarships are open to Pakistani applicants because eligibility is usually based on academic merit rather than nationality. The Orange Knowledge Programme has historically included Pakistan, and university awards such as LEXS, the Amsterdam Excellence Scholarship, and the Twente Scholarship accept applicants worldwide. For a broader search, see fully funded scholarships for Pakistani students and browse all Netherlands scholarships.

How to evaluate a Netherlands scholarship offer

  1. Confirm what is covered: tuition only, or tuition plus living stipend, travel, and insurance.
  2. Check whether the award is conditional on admission or includes guaranteed admission.
  3. Compare the award with the IND proof-of-funds requirement; a partial award may still require you to show additional savings.
  4. Verify the exact deadline and selection criteria on the official provider page, not on third-party sites.

Admission Requirements

Dutch universities assess your academic background, English proficiency, and programme fit. Requirements vary by level and institution, but the general rules are:

Bachelor's programmes

  • Academic: a secondary school diploma equivalent to the Dutch VWO (for research universities) or HAVO/HBO (for universities of applied sciences). Some programmes require specific subjects such as mathematics, physics, or economics.
  • English: IELTS 6.0-6.5, TOEFL iBT 80-90, or Cambridge B2 First/C1 Advanced, depending on the programme.
  • Selection: numerus fixus programmes (such as medicine, dentistry, and some psychology programmes) require a ranking or lottery-based selection, so apply before the fixed deadline.

Master's programmes

  • Academic: a relevant bachelor's degree from a recognised institution. Most universities require a GPA equivalent to roughly 3.0 on a 4.0 scale (7.5/10 or 60-70%), though requirements vary.
  • English: IELTS 6.5-7.0, TOEFL iBT 90-100, or Cambridge C1 Advanced, with higher scores for law, journalism, and some business programmes.
  • Tests: select programmes, especially at business schools such as RSM and Maastricht, require the GRE or GMAT. Check the programme page.
  • Work experience: certain MBA and specialised master's programmes require professional experience.

PhD programmes

  • Academic: a relevant master's degree, often with a research component.
  • Research proposal: you must find a supervisor whose research matches your topic and agree on a research plan before applying.
  • Funding: most PhDs are salaried positions; apply through the university's vacancy portal rather than a general application form.

Because every programme sets its own requirements, always read the official admission page for your target university. Our university admissions guide and how to write an SOP resources cover the application documents in detail.

Study in Netherlands Without IELTS

Yes, you can study in the Netherlands without IELTS if you meet one of the accepted alternatives or exemptions. Dutch universities recognise several equivalent tests and waive the requirement in specific cases:

  • TOEFL iBT and Cambridge English (CAE/CPE): the most widely accepted alternatives across all Dutch universities.
  • Duolingo English Test: accepted by an increasing number of institutions, including the University of Twente, VU Amsterdam, and several applied-sciences universities. Always verify on the official programme page.
  • PTE Academic: now accepted by several Dutch universities as an alternative to IELTS.
  • English-medium instruction exemption: if your previous degree or secondary education was taught entirely in English, many universities waive the language test. You usually need to prove this with a Medium of Instruction letter from your institution.
  • Nationality exemption: applicants from the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, and other English-speaking countries are typically exempt.
  • University interview: a few programmes allow a personal interview in place of a test score.

Because acceptance of alternatives differs between universities, check the language requirements section of your target programme before booking any test. Our scholarships without IELTS guide lists funded opportunities that do not require a test.

Student Visa Requirements

Non-EU/EEA students need two immigration documents to study in the Netherlands: an entry visa (MVV) if your nationality requires one, and a residence permit for study purposes (VVR). In almost all cases, your university acts as the sponsor and applies to the IND on your behalf.

The MVV and residence permit process

  1. Receive an admission letter (Toelatingsbesluit) from a Dutch institution.
  2. Accept the offer and complete any enrolment steps and fee payments required by the university.
  3. Provide proof of funds (roughly €1,175/month for 12 months), a valid passport, and your personal details so the university can submit the application.
  4. The university applies to the IND for your MVV and/or residence permit. You do not apply yourself.
  5. Once approved, the IND notifies the Dutch embassy in your home country, where you collect the MVV sticker (valid 90 days) and then travel to the Netherlands.
  6. After arrival, book an appointment with the IND to collect your residence permit card within two weeks of arriving.

Processing time: the IND's official decision period is up to 90 days, but applications submitted through universities are usually processed in 2-4 weeks. The application fee for a study residence permit is roughly €210 (updated annually). Students are not required to show a TB certificate or police clearance for most nationalities, but this can vary by country of origin.

Schengen travel

Your MVV gives you 90 days of Schengen entry, and the residence permit then allows you to travel freely across the Schengen Area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. For authoritative guidance on the study residence permit and MVV, use the official IND residence permit pages and the Study in Holland visa section.

Required Documents

Prepare a complete, organised document pack early, because transcripts, legalisation, and translations take time. The exact list depends on your programme, but most applications require:

  • Valid passport: valid for at least six months beyond your intended stay.
  • Academic transcripts and diploma: certified copies of your secondary school or university records, translated into English if required.
  • English proficiency proof: IELTS, TOEFL, Cambridge, Duolingo, or a medium-of-instruction letter.
  • Motivation letter: explain why the programme and university fit your goals.
  • Curriculum vitae (CV): especially for master's and PhD applications.
  • References: usually two academic or professional recommendation letters.
  • Proof of funds: bank statements showing you can cover tuition and living costs.
  • Health insurance: arrange coverage before or shortly after arrival.
  • Additional tests and legalisation: the GRE or GMAT for some business programmes, plus apostilled or legalised documents for certain countries, including Pakistan.

Application Process

The application process for the Netherlands follows a standard, step-by-step flow. Work through it in order and build in buffer time for documents and deadlines.

  1. Shortlist programmes: use the Study in Holland portal and the Nuffic website to compare programmes, fees, and deadlines. Filter by level, field, and English-taught status.
  2. Check requirements and eligibility: confirm the English scores, GPA, specific subjects, and any tests (GRE/GMAT) for each programme.
  3. Prepare your documents: transcripts, diploma, motivation letter, CV, references, and any legalised translations.
  4. Register in Studielink: the Netherlands uses Studielink as the national enrolment system. Every Dutch university requires you to create an account and submit an enrolment request there, in addition to the university's own portal.
  5. Submit the university application: complete the online application form with your documents before the non-EU deadline (usually January to April for September intake).
  6. Apply for scholarships: submit separate scholarship applications (Holland Scholarship, LEXS, Amsterdam Excellence Scholarship, and others) in parallel with admission.
  7. Receive and accept your offer: when you get a conditional or unconditional offer, accept it and complete enrolment steps (payment, documents).
  8. Start the IND process: your university applies to the IND for your MVV and residence permit after you accept the offer.
  9. Arrange housing, insurance, and flights: book accommodation early, arrange health insurance, and plan your arrival for the university introduction week.
  10. Travel and register: collect your MVV at the embassy, travel to the Netherlands, register at the municipality, and collect your residence permit card.

Can International Students Work?

Yes. International students in the Netherlands can work part-time while studying, but the rules depend on your nationality and your residence permit conditions.

  • Non-EU students: you may work up to 16 hours per week during the academic year and full-time during June, July, and August. Your employer generally needs to obtain a work permit (TWV) from the UWV before you start.
  • EU/EEA and Swiss students: you can work freely without a work permit under the same rules as Dutch citizens.
  • Health insurance: if you work and your employer deducts Dutch payroll taxes, you are usually required to take out Dutch basic health insurance in addition to any student insurance.
  • Common jobs: retail, hospitality, delivery, tutoring, campus roles, and English-teaching assistant positions are the most common part-time jobs for international students. Fluency in Dutch opens many more roles.

Keep in mind that a part-time job typically covers only part of your living costs. Budget carefully, and do not rely on work income when you apply for your visa, because the IND requires documented proof of funds.

Post Study Work Visa

The Netherlands is one of the most generous countries in Europe for graduates. After finishing your degree, you can stay for a year through the Orientation Year for Highly Educated Persons, commonly called the zoekjaar.

  • Who qualifies: graduates of Dutch research universities, universities of applied sciences, and PhD candidates, as well as graduates of foreign universities ranked in the global top 200 who completed their degree within the previous three years.
  • What it allows: a one-year residence permit to work freely (no work permit needed), search for a job, start a business, or do an internship.
  • After the orientation year: when you find a job that meets the income threshold, you can switch to the Highly Skilled Migrant (kennismigrant) residence permit, which can lead to permanent residence and, eventually, Dutch citizenship.
  • How to apply: apply through the IND before your study residence permit expires, ideally during the final months of your programme.

This pathway makes studying in the Netherlands particularly attractive for international students who want to build a European career. For the official rules, see the IND orientation year information.

Intakes

Dutch universities mainly admit students twice a year: a large September intake and a smaller February intake. Some programmes offer rolling admissions.

  • September intake (main): the deadline for non-EU students is usually between 1 October and 1 April, depending on the programme and university. TU Delft, UvA, and Erasmus typically use deadlines of 1 April, 15 January, or 1 February for popular programmes.
  • February intake (secondary): available for many master's programmes and some bachelor's programmes. Deadlines for non-EU applicants usually fall between 1 November and 1 December.
  • Numerus fixus programmes: programmes with a limited number of places (medicine, dentistry, some psychology and business programmes) have fixed deadlines that are often months earlier and use a selection procedure.

Always confirm the exact deadline for your specific programme on the university website, because non-EU deadlines are significantly earlier than EU deadlines.

Common Mistakes

  • Missing the non-EU deadline: assuming the same deadlines as EU students. Non-EU deadlines are usually months earlier.
  • Ignoring Studielink: forgetting that every Dutch university requires enrolment through the national Studielink system in addition to the university portal.
  • Applying without checking programme-specific requirements: not every programme accepts your GPA, subject mix, or English test, so verify before applying.
  • Waiting for scholarship results before applying for admission: admission and scholarships are separate tracks; apply for both at the same time.
  • Underestimating the housing crisis: leaving accommodation until after arrival almost guarantees high prices or no room at all.
  • Not budgeting for the full proof-of-funds amount: the IND requires documented funds even if you expect to work part-time.
  • Forgetting health insurance: uninsured treatment in the Netherlands is expensive, and insurance is mandatory once you work.
  • Submitting a generic motivation letter: programmes explicitly look for evidence of fit; tailor every essay to the programme.
  • Missing the orientation year window: apply for the zoekjaar before your study permit expires, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What IELTS score is required to study in the Netherlands?

Most Dutch universities require IELTS 6.0-6.5 for bachelor's programmes and 6.5-7.0 for master's programmes. Requirements vary by university and course. TOEFL iBT, Cambridge English, and (for some institutions) Duolingo are accepted alternatives. Always confirm the exact band on the official programme page.

How much does it cost to study in the Netherlands per year?

Non-EU/EEA students typically pay €8,000-€15,000 per year for bachelor's and €10,000-€20,000 for master's tuition. Living costs add roughly €1,000-€1,500 per month including accommodation, food, transport, and insurance. EU/EEA students pay statutory fees of about €2,530-€2,601 per year.

Can international students work while studying in the Netherlands?

Yes. Non-EU students may work up to 16 hours per week during the academic year and full-time during June, July, and August. A work permit (TWV) is usually required, arranged by the employer. Students who are also working should register for Dutch basic health insurance.

Can I study in the Netherlands without IELTS?

Yes. Universities accept TOEFL iBT, Cambridge English (CAE/CPE), and in some cases Duolingo English Test or PTE Academic. You may also get an exemption if your previous degree was taught fully in English. Check each programme's language policy, as acceptance of alternatives varies by institution.

What scholarships are available for international students in the Netherlands?

Key options include the Holland Scholarship (a one-off grant of €5,000 for non-EEA students), Erasmus Mundus Joint Master's scholarships (full tuition plus stipend), university awards such as the Amsterdam Excellence Scholarship, Radboud Scholarship, Leiden University Excellence Scholarship (LEXS), and University of Twente Scholarship, and paid PhD positions.

Can Pakistani students study in the Netherlands?

Yes. Pakistani students can apply to Dutch universities and to many scholarships that accept non-EEA applicants, including the Holland Scholarship and university excellence awards. They must meet admission, English language, and proof-of-funds requirements, and apply for an entry visa (MVV) and residence permit through the IND.

How long does the Netherlands student visa process take?

Once your university submits the residence permit application to the IND, processing usually takes about 2-4 weeks, though the IND's official maximum is up to 90 days. The university typically applies on your behalf after you accept your offer, pay any fees, and provide proof of funds and a valid passport.

What is the post-study work visa in the Netherlands?

Graduates of Dutch universities can apply for the Orientation Year (zoekjaar), a one-year residence permit to work, start a business, or search for a job without a separate work permit. After finding employment, graduates can switch to a Highly Skilled Migrant residence permit.

What are the main intakes at Dutch universities?

The main intake is September, with non-EU application deadlines typically between 1 October and 1 April, depending on the programme. A smaller February intake exists for many programmes, with deadlines around 1 November to 1 December. Some programmes offer rolling or multiple intakes.

Which are the cheapest cities to study in the Netherlands?

Groningen, Enschede (University of Twente), Nijmegen (Radboud University), Maastricht, and Tilburg generally have lower living costs than Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and The Hague. Amsterdam is the most expensive, with shared accommodation often costing €700-€1,000 per month.

Do I need to speak Dutch to study in the Netherlands?

No. The Netherlands offers over 2,100 English-taught degree programmes, and Dutch people have high English proficiency, so you can study and live comfortably in English. Learning basic Dutch helps with part-time jobs, housing, and integration, but it is not required for admission.

How much money do I need to prove for a Netherlands student visa?

For a residence permit, the IND requires proof of sufficient funds of roughly €1,175 per month for 12 months, approximately €14,000 for one year, covering living costs (tuition is separate). The required monthly amount is updated periodically, so confirm the current figure on the official IND website.

Can I bring my family with me on a Netherlands student visa?

Yes, but only if you are studying a full-time bachelor's, master's, or PhD. Your partner and children can apply for residence permits to join you. Family members must meet income and insurance requirements, and they generally do not have unrestricted work rights unless specific conditions apply.

Are PhD positions in the Netherlands paid?

Yes. Most PhD positions at Dutch research universities are salaried employment contracts that include a monthly salary, social security, and employee benefits. Some are funded through fellowships or scholarships. PhD candidates are considered staff rather than students for most purposes.

What documents are required to study in the Netherlands?

Commonly required documents are a valid passport, academic transcripts and diploma, proof of English proficiency, a motivation letter, CV, references, proof of sufficient funds, and health insurance. Some programmes require an apostilled or legalised degree and additional tests such as the GRE or GMAT.

Is it worth studying in the Netherlands as an international student?

For most international students, yes. The Netherlands combines globally ranked English-taught universities, relatively affordable tuition compared with the UK or US, a strong job market, and a one-year post-study orientation visa. Careful budgeting for housing and costs makes it a strong value destination.

When should I start applying to study in the Netherlands?

Start 12-15 months before your intended September intake. Research programmes, take your English test, and gather documents early. Submit your university application via Studielink and the university portal by the non-EU deadline, usually between January and April, then apply for scholarships and begin the IND process after you receive an offer.

What are the English-taught universities in the Netherlands?

Essentially all Dutch research universities and universities of applied sciences offer English-taught programmes. Leading options include the University of Amsterdam, TU Delft, Utrecht University, Leiden University, Erasmus University Rotterdam, University of Groningen, Radboud University, Maastricht University, and Wageningen University & Research.

  • Have I registered in Studielink?
  • Have I taken my English test or confirmed an exemption?
  • Have I applied for scholarships in the Netherlands?
  • Have I submitted proof of funds for the IND?
  • Have I arranged housing and health insurance?
  • Have I planned my application for the orientation year?

Official Resources and References

For accurate, up-to-date information, always rely on official Dutch government and university sources:

  • Study in Holland: official government portal for international students
  • Nuffic: Dutch organisation for internationalisation in education
  • IND (Immigration and Naturalisation Service): official immigration and residence permit information
  • Holland Scholarship: official scholarship website
  • Erasmus Mundus: European Commission scholarship catalogue
  • Erasmus+: European Commission Erasmus+ programme

Important Disclaimer

This guide is for informational purposes only. Tuition fees, living costs, scholarship availability, visa requirements, and immigration policies in the Netherlands change regularly. Always verify the current information with official sources such as the IND, Nuffic, Study in Holland, and your chosen university before making any decision. OpportunityNest does not provide visa advice or immigration consultancy services. For official visa information, visit the IND website or consult a registered immigration adviser.
