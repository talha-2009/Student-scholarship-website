Study in Germany: Complete Guide for International Students

Last updated August 4, 2026 · Reviewed by Abdullah Ijaz Abbasi · 25 min read

Germany is one of the world’s most attractive study destinations, offering world-class education at little to no tuition cost at public universities. With over 400 higher education institutions, strong industry connections, and more English-taught programmes every year, it provides exceptional value. This guide covers everything you need to know.

This 2026 guide covers why Germany is worth considering, its strongest universities, real costs for Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg, DAAD and foundation scholarships, admission requirements, the student visa, work rights, and intakes. Use the table of contents below to jump to any section.

Why Study in Germany?

Germany is Europe’s largest economy and hosts roughly 380,000 international students, making it the third most popular study destination after the United States and the United Kingdom. The combination of affordable education and strong employment prospects is unmatched in Western Europe.

  • Tuition-free public universities: Most states abolished tuition fees in 2014, so public universities charge nothing; you only pay a semester contribution of EUR 100-350.
  • English-taught programmes: More than 2,000 bachelor’s and master’s programmes are taught entirely in English, so you can study without German first.
  • Engineering and automotive strength: Germany is home to Volkswagen, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Siemens, Bosch, SAP, and BASF, and universities work closely with industry, so internships and graduate jobs are realistic outcomes.
  • DAAD support: The German Academic Exchange Service (DAAD) is the world’s largest funder of international student exchange, supporting thousands of students every year.
  • Safety and quality of life: German cities rank high in global liveability surveys, with reliable transport, affordable healthcare, and low violent-crime rates.
  • Post-study work rights: After graduation you can stay for 18 months to find a job, with a path to the EU Blue Card.

Top German Universities

Germany has no single national ranking, but several institutions appear consistently in the global top 100, and all seven below are recognised as Universities of Excellence.

  • Technical University of Munich (TUM): Germany’s top-ranked university and a TU9 member, with strengths in engineering, computer science, and natural sciences.
  • Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München (LMU): A prestigious research university ranked highly in physics, life sciences, medicine, and the humanities.
  • Heidelberg University: Germany’s oldest university (founded 1386), world-renowned for medicine, molecular biosciences, and law.
  • Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT): A technical university merged with a national research centre, strong in engineering, computer science, and energy research.
  • TU Berlin: Strong in engineering, architecture, urban planning, and natural sciences, in the heart of a startup hub.
  • RWTH Aachen: One of Europe’s leading technical universities, famous for mechanical engineering and automotive research.
  • Freie Universität Berlin: Known for the humanities, social sciences, political science, and economics.

Choosing a university: Rankings matter less than programme fit. Compare whether your course is taught in English or German, the semester contribution, housing availability, and industry links. A technical university partnered with your target employers can beat a higher-ranked general university.

Cost of Studying in Germany

Tuition at public universities in most states is free, even for international students. You pay only a semester contribution of EUR 100-350, covering administration, student services, and — in most cities — a semester ticket for public transport. Exceptions are Baden-Württemberg, which charges non-EU students about EUR 1,500 per semester, and private universities with their own fees. Living costs are your real budget item and vary by city.

Scholarships for International Students in Germany

Germany is one of the most scholarship-friendly countries, with funding from the federal government, universities, foundations, and companies. Below are the programmes international students apply for most often.

  • DAAD Scholarships: The German Academic Exchange Service funds dozens of programmes at all levels. The DAAD Study Scholarship supports a full master’s degree, the EPOS programme funds development-related postgraduate courses, the Helmut Schmidt Programme targets public policy and good governance, and the DAAD STEM Scholarship supports science and technology students. Most DAAD awards cover a monthly stipend, health insurance, and travel.
  • Deutschlandstipendium: A EUR 300 monthly stipend funded half by the government and half by private donors, awarded on merit with no repayment.
  • Heinrich Böll Foundation: Offers scholarships to master’s and PhD students who support the foundation’s values of ecology, democracy, and human rights.
  • Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung: Awards scholarships to outstanding international students with leadership potential and strong academic records.
  • Friedrich Ebert Stiftung: Supports students committed to social democracy and social justice, including many applicants from developing countries.
  • BMW Group Change Maker Fellowship: A newer programme for students working on sustainability and future mobility. See the fellowship details.

Best strategy: DAAD applications are competitive and deadlines fall about a year early, so prepare a strong motivation letter and also apply for university awards once admitted. Browse all scholarships in Germany and our list of top fully funded scholarships.

Admission Requirements

Requirements vary by university and programme, but the following are the general expectations for international applicants.

Academic requirements

  • Undergraduate: Your secondary school certificate must be equivalent to the German Abitur. Check your country’s status in the Anabin database to see whether you can apply directly or need a one-year Studienkolleg preparatory course.
  • Master’s: A relevant bachelor’s degree, usually with a minimum grade of 2.5 or better on the German scale. Some programmes require work experience or a portfolio.
  • PhD: A strong master’s degree and a research proposal; many PhD positions in Germany are funded jobs rather than scholarships.

Language requirements

  • German-taught programmes: DSH-2 or TestDaF (usually level 4 in all sections). A B2 certificate is the practical minimum for daily life and internships.
  • English-taught programmes: IELTS 6.0-6.5 overall or an equivalent TOEFL score (typically 80-90 iBT).

Required documents

  • Certified academic transcripts and degree certificates
  • APS certificate — mandatory for applicants from China, India, and Vietnam before they can apply or obtain a visa
  • English or German language certificate
  • Motivation letter — see our motivation letter guide
  • CV and two reference letters for graduate programmes
  • Valid passport copy

What is Anabin? Anabin is the official German database for evaluating foreign qualifications. Enter your country and certificate to see if it is equivalent to the Abitur, whether you need a Studienkolleg year, or whether your bachelor’s is recognised for master’s admission. Most universities and uni-assist use it.

Student Visa for Germany

Students from EU/EEA countries need no visa. Everyone else must apply for a national student visa (type D) at the German embassy or consulate in their home country — well before the course starts, because processing takes 6 to 12 weeks. See our full student visa guide.

  1. Get your admission letter: You need a confirmed place at a German university before you can apply for the visa.
  2. Open a blocked account: Deposit EUR 11,208 into a blocked account (Sperrkonto) with a provider such as Fintiba or Expatrio, which releases about EUR 934 per month.
  3. Arrange health insurance: Public student insurance with TK, AOK, Barmer, or DAK costs about EUR 110-120 per month for students under 30, and coverage must start before enrolment.
  4. Book an appointment: Reserve a visa appointment at your embassy or consulate as early as possible — slots in many countries book out months ahead.
  5. Submit your documents: Admission letter, passport, blocked account statement, health insurance confirmation, language certificate, APS certificate (where required), and proof of prior qualifications.
  6. Attend the interview: You may be asked about your study plans, funding, and intentions after the degree.

After graduation

Once you complete your degree, you can apply for an 18-month job seeker residence permit and work without restriction while you search. When you find a job related to your qualification, you can switch to an EU Blue Card or a residence permit for qualified professionals.

Note: The blocked account figure is set by the German government and reviewed periodically, so confirm the current amount on the official embassy website before transferring.

Working While Studying in Germany

International students from outside the EU/EEA may work while they study, subject to Germany’s famous day limit.

  • The 120/240 rule: You may work 120 full days or 240 half days per calendar year. A full day is any shift, even a short one, so two half-day jobs consume the allowance twice as fast.
  • Pay: Typical student jobs pay EUR 12-14 per hour, above Germany’s statutory minimum wage.
  • Semester breaks: You can work full-time during breaks, but the annual day allowance still applies.
  • EU/EEA students: No day limit applies; they can work like German students.

Popular part-time jobs

  • Research assistant (HiWi) positions at your own university — paid, relevant to your field, and convenient
  • Hospitality: waiter, barista, kitchen assistant, café staff
  • Retail and warehouse work during peak seasons
  • Tutoring, especially if you are strong in mathematics or science
  • Delivery and courier work for apps and local businesses

Working 10 to 15 hours a week can cover food and leisure, while a research assistant role adds real CV experience. But your studies come first — a failed semester affects your visa and employability. For job-hunting support, see our guide to paid internships.

Intakes and Application Timeline

German universities run two intakes a year; winter is by far the larger one.

  • Winter semester: Starts in October and is the main intake, including for most English-taught master’s courses. Deadlines typically fall between May and July.
  • Summer semester: Starts in April. Fewer programmes begin then, and many are German-taught. Deadlines usually fall in January.
  • uni-assist: Many universities require international applications to go through uni-assist, the central service that checks whether your certificates match German qualifications. Expect about EUR 75 for the first application and EUR 30 for each additional one in the same semester.

Recommended timeline

  • 12-18 months before: Choose your programme and university; check the Anabin database; start language preparation.
  • 9-12 months before: Apply for DAAD and foundation scholarships; take IELTS/TOEFL or TestDaF; obtain your APS certificate if required.
  • 6-9 months before: Submit university applications (often via uni-assist); request references and documents.
  • 3-6 months before: Receive your admission, open the blocked account, arrange health insurance, and book your visa appointment.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is studying in Germany really free for international students?

Yes, for most public universities. Since most states abolished tuition fees in 2014, you only pay a semester contribution of EUR 100 to 350. Exceptions are Baden-Württemberg (about EUR 1,500 per semester for non-EU students) and private universities.

How much money do I need to show for a German student visa?

You must prove financial resources of at least EUR 11,208 per year, usually with a blocked account (Sperrkonto). Scholarships, bank guarantees, or a sponsor’s declaration are also accepted, and the figure is reviewed periodically.

What is the semester contribution and what does it cover?

The semester contribution of EUR 100 to 350 per semester is not tuition. It covers student services, the administration fee, and in most cities a semester ticket for public transport.

Can I work while studying in Germany?

Yes. Non-EU students may work 120 full days or 240 half days per year, earning roughly EUR 12 to 14 per hour. Campus research assistant jobs and work during semester breaks are the most common options.

What language test do I need for German universities?

German-taught programmes require DSH-2 or TestDaF (level 4 in all sections). English-taught programmes require IELTS 6.0 to 6.5 or an equivalent TOEFL score; some universities accept other tests.

Do I need an APS certificate to apply?

Students from China, India, and Vietnam must get an APS certificate at home before applying, because German embassies there will not accept visa applications without it. Check Anabin for your country.

Which scholarships can international students apply for in Germany?

DAAD funds the largest range of fully funded master’s and PhD programmes, including the Study Scholarship, EPOS, Helmut Schmidt, and STEM awards. Political foundations such as Heinrich Böll, Konrad-Adenauer, and Friedrich Ebert, plus the Deutschlandstipendium (EUR 300 per month), are also worth applying to.

Can I stay in Germany after graduation to find a job?

Yes. After graduation you can apply for an 18-month job seeker residence permit and work without restriction. Once you find a job related to your degree, you can switch to an EU Blue Card or a residence permit for qualified professionals.

How long does a German student visa application take?

Processing at the embassy usually takes 6 to 12 weeks, so apply as soon as you receive your admission letter. You will need admission, proof of finances, health insurance, a passport, and your APS certificate if required.

Do I need health insurance to study in Germany?

Yes, health insurance is compulsory before enrolment. Students under 30 can join the public system (TK, AOK, Barmer, DAK) for about EUR 110 to 120 per month, covering doctor visits, hospital care, and most medications.

Study in Germany Checklist

  • Have I chosen between a German-taught and an English-taught programme?
  • Have I confirmed my qualifications are recognised in the Anabin database?
  • Have I obtained my APS certificate (China, India, or Vietnam)?
  • Have I taken the required language test (IELTS/TOEFL or DSH/TestDaF)?
  • Have I applied for DAAD and foundation scholarships before deadlines?
  • Have I opened a blocked account with EUR 11,208?
  • Have I arranged public health insurance (EUR 110-120 per month)?
  • Have I submitted my student visa application at the embassy?
  • Have I budgeted for rent, food, and the semester contribution?
  • Have I arranged accommodation before arrival?
