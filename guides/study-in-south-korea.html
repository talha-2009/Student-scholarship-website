Study in South Korea: Complete Guide for International Students

Last updated August 4, 2026 · Reviewed by Abdullah Ijaz Abbasi · 25 min read

South Korea has rapidly become one of Asia's most attractive study destinations. A country that rebuilt itself after the Korean War is now home to global technology giants such as Samsung, LG, and Hyundai, and its universities climb international rankings every year. Seoul National University, KAIST, and the SKY universities now sit alongside the best institutions in the United States and Europe, yet tuition is a fraction of what you would pay in Australia, the UK, or North America. For Pakistani, South Asian, and other international students, South Korea offers world-class education, generous government scholarships, a famously safe society, and a cultural wave — from K-pop to Korean cinema — that has made the country famous worldwide.

This complete 2026 guide covers everything you need to know about studying in South Korea: the top universities including the SKY group, the Korean Government Scholarship Program (KGSP, now branded GKS), a cost comparison of national and private universities, scholarships beyond GKS, admission requirements and TOPIK, the D-2 student visa and work rules, and the March and September intakes. Why Study in South Korea?

South Korea invests heavily in education — around five percent of GDP — and the results show in global rankings, research output, and graduate employability. It has one of the highest tertiary enrolment rates in the world, and because the Ministry of Education regulates its roughly 300 universities and colleges, quality is consistent. Degrees are recognised worldwide, English-taught programmes are common at the top universities, and the government funds some of the most generous scholarships in Asia.

  • Global technology leader: Samsung Electronics, LG Electronics, SK Hynix, and Hyundai Motor Company design the world's smartphones, memory chips, and electric vehicles. Studying here puts you inside that ecosystem.
  • K-pop, Hallyu, and culture: BTS, BLACKPINK, streamed K-dramas, and Oscar-winning films like Parasite have made Seoul a cultural capital and a magnet for media careers.
  • Affordable tuition: Fees are far lower than in the US, UK, or Australia, and scholarships frequently reduce the cost to near zero.
  • Safety: South Korea has one of the lowest violent crime rates among developed nations, and cities are clean and safe to walk through at night.
  • Fast internet: Korea consistently ranks among the fastest countries in the world for internet speeds — a real advantage for research and online work.
  • Great transport: Seoul's public transport is among the world's best, and the high-speed KTX rail links Seoul, Daejeon, and Busan in under three hours.

Did you know? The "SKY" universities — Seoul National University, Korea University, and Yonsei University — have been so dominant in Korean recruitment that the term is now everyday shorthand for prestige, similar to the Ivy League.

Top Universities in South Korea

South Korea has a two-tier system of national (public) and private universities. National universities are government-funded and charge lower tuition; private universities are often more expensive but include some of the country's most prestigious names. The SKY universities — Seoul National University, Korea University, and Yonsei University — remain the most sought-after. SNU is the flagship national university and consistently ranks first in Korea. For science and technology, KAIST and POSTECH are internationally renowned and offer many fully funded opportunities for graduate researchers, while SKKU and Hanyang are excellent practical choices with strong industry links.

Choosing a university: Do not choose purely on prestige. Compare tuition, scholarships, whether your programme is taught in English or Korean, location and living costs, and international student support. A strong scholarship at SKKU or Hanyang can be a smarter financial decision than full private tuition elsewhere.

Korean Government Scholarship (KGSP / GKS)

The Korean Government Scholarship Program (KGSP), now officially branded the Global Korea Scholarship (GKS), is the flagship funding route for international students, administered by the National Institute for International Education (NIIED). It is one of the most generous national scholarships in Asia and has brought thousands of students from more than 150 countries to Korea. If you are serious about studying in Korea, this is your first priority.

What GKS covers

  • Full tuition fees for the entire degree programme
  • A monthly stipend of approximately KRW 1,000,000 to cover living expenses
  • Round-trip airfare between your home country and Korea
  • National Health Insurance for the duration of your stay
  • A settlement allowance when you first arrive
  • One year of intensive Korean language training before your degree begins

Embassy track vs university track

There are two application routes, and you must choose one — you cannot apply through both in the same cycle.

  • Embassy track: You apply through the Korean embassy or consulate in your home country. Each country receives a small quota, so competition is national. The embassy screens applications, interviews candidates, and forwards the strongest to NIIED. Deadlines vary by country but usually fall in early to mid-March, right after the global window opens in February.
  • University track: You apply directly to a Korean university that participates in GKS. The university shortlists applicants and nominates them to NIIED, with each university setting its own deadline, typically between March and April.

Selection criteria

NIIED and the universities evaluate academic grades, language ability (TOPIK for Korean-taught programmes, or IELTS/TOEFL for English-taught programmes), the quality of your essays and study plan, recommendation letters, and an interview. A TOPIK certificate is not mandatory for every track, but it strongly strengthens your application, and some universities give bonus points for TOPIK Level 4 or higher.

Key dates: GKS applications typically open in February, but embassy deadlines vary by country and can be as early as March, so prepare your documents months in advance. Scholars normally enter Korea in the fall semester. Read the full eligibility details on the dedicated Global Korea Scholarship (GKS) opportunity page.

Cost of Studying in South Korea

South Korea is far more affordable than Australia, the UK, or the US. Tuition is charged per semester, with two semesters per academic year. National universities charge less than private universities, which include most top names apart from SNU and KAIST.

Dormitory vs private housing

  • University dormitories: The cheapest and safest option, and the first choice for most international students in year one. Rooms are usually shared and demand is high, so apply as soon as you are admitted.
  • Private housing: One-room apartments and goshiwon (tiny studio rooms) are common. Private rentals often require a deposit (key money) on top of monthly rent, so dormitories are usually better for short stays.
  • Homestay: Living with a Korean family is a less common but excellent way to improve your Korean quickly.

A self-funded year in Korea typically costs between KRW 14,000,000 and 26,000,000 including tuition and living costs — a fraction of the equivalent cost in most Western countries. For budgeting ideas, see our guide to studying abroad on a budget.

Scholarships Beyond GKS

GKS is the best-known award, but it is far from the only one. Government support plus institutional generosity means many international students study at a heavily discounted rate, and some at no cost at all.

  • University merit and international scholarships: Almost every Korean university offers scholarships for international students. KAIST provides full tuition plus a monthly stipend to many of its international graduate researchers, while SNU, Yonsei, Korea University, SKKU, and Hanyang run tuition waiver programmes covering 30-100% of fees for top applicants.
  • TOPIK-based tuition reductions: Many universities automatically reduce or waive tuition when you hold a TOPIK certificate — for example, a 50% waiver for Level 4 and a 100% waiver for Level 5 or 6 at some institutions. A strong TOPIK score is one of the most valuable investments you can make before applying.
  • Graduate research and teaching assistantships: Master's and PhD students can earn a monthly salary through research (RA) or teaching (TA) assistant positions, which often covers living costs on top of a tuition waiver.
  • Korean language training scholarships: Beyond the GKS language year, the government and individual universities fund intensive Korean language programmes, and many degree scholarships include a language-training semester as part of the package.

Strategy: Apply for GKS first because it is the most competitive and closes earliest. Simultaneously apply to at least two universities for their own scholarships, and register for TOPIK even if you plan to study in English — it unlocks automatic tuition reductions. Admission Requirements

Requirements vary by university and programme, but the general expectations for international applicants are consistent across Korean universities.

Academic requirements

  • Undergraduate: A high school diploma or equivalent (FSc / intermediate / A-Levels for Pakistani applicants), usually with a strong academic record. Some universities consider an entrance exam.
  • Master's: A bachelor's degree in a relevant field with a good GPA (typically 2.5-3.0 on a 4.0 scale, or 60-70% aggregate). Some competitive programmes ask for work experience.
  • PhD: A relevant master's degree, a research proposal, and usually a supervisor match before you apply.

Language requirements

  • Korean-taught programmes: TOPIK Level 3 or above is the standard minimum, and competitive universities require Level 4 for humanities and social sciences. GKS scholars reach Level 3-4 through the language-training year before their degree starts.
  • English-taught programmes: IELTS (typically 6.0-6.5) or TOEFL iBT (typically 80-100) is required. Universities that teach in English, such as Yonsei's Underwood International College and KAIST, waive the test for applicants from English-medium universities.

Documents you will need

  • Certified academic transcripts and certificates
  • TOPIK certificate or IELTS/TOEFL results (whichever your programme requires)
  • A personal statement and study plan — see our personal statement guide
  • Two to three recommendation letters from teachers or professors
  • A copy of your passport and, for self-funded applicants, proof of financial capacity
  • A portfolio for art, design, music, and architecture programmes

See our university admissions guide for a step-by-step walkthrough of assembling your application.

Tip: Many Korean admissions are document-based rather than exam-based, so your statement of purpose and recommendation letters carry real weight. Tailor each one to the specific university and department, and have them reviewed before you submit.

Student Visa (D-2) and Work Rules

Degree-seeking international students require the D-2 student visa, issued through a Korean embassy or consulate in your home country. It is usually granted for one year at a time, with renewals handled by the immigration office once you arrive.

How to apply for the D-2 visa

  1. Receive your certificate of admission: Your university issues this after you are accepted and confirm your place.
  2. Gather your documents: Valid passport, visa application form, certificate of admission, academic documents, a photo, the visa fee, and proof of financial capacity such as a bank balance covering your first year of tuition and living costs.
  3. Submit at the embassy: Processing usually takes a few weeks, so do not book flights until your visa is granted.
  4. Register after arrival: Within 90 days of landing you must visit the local immigration office to apply for an Alien Registration Card (ARC), which doubles as your residence ID and is needed to open a bank account or get a phone plan.

Working while studying

Part-time work is allowed on a D-2 visa, but it requires permission and comes with limits:

  • You must obtain work permission from the immigration office and approval from your university.
  • During the semester you may work about 20 hours per week; during official vacation periods the limit is removed.
  • Jobs are restricted to approved categories such as retail, restaurants and cafés, tutoring, and on-campus roles, and most categories require a TOPIK score.
  • Work must not compromise your studies — universities and immigration review your academic progress, and poor grades can jeopardise your visa and scholarship.

Staying after graduation

After finishing your degree, you can apply for a D-10 job seeker visa, which gives you six months to a year to find a job in Korea. Once employed in a role relevant to your degree, you switch to an E-7 work visa, and after several years of residence many graduates qualify for an F-2 residence visa — the standard path toward long-term settlement. Intakes and Application Timeline

Korean universities operate on a two-semester calendar with two main intakes per year:

  • Spring semester (March): The main intake for most programmes. Applications typically open in July and close around September of the previous year.
  • Fall semester (September): The second major intake and the one used by most GKS scholars. Applications typically open in March and close in April-May.

Korean language programmes run more frequently, usually with four intakes per year in March, June, September, and December.

Recommended timeline

  • 12 months before: Shortlist universities; choose between the embassy and university track; start language or English test preparation.
  • 9-10 months before: Register for TOPIK or IELTS/TOEFL; gather and attest transcripts; begin your personal statement and study plan.
  • 6-7 months before: Submit GKS applications (embassy deadlines vary from March) and university applications; secure recommendation letters.
  • 3-4 months before: Receive admission results; apply for the D-2 visa; arrange dormitory accommodation; book flights.
  • On arrival: Register for your ARC, open a bank account, and attend orientation.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

How much does it cost to study in South Korea?

Tuition at national (public) universities ranges from KRW 2,000,000 to 6,000,000 per semester, and at private universities from KRW 3,500,000 to 9,000,000 per semester. Monthly living costs in Seoul are typically KRW 600,000 to 1,000,000, while other cities are cheaper.

What does the Korean Government Scholarship Program (GKS/KGSP) cover?

The GKS (formerly KGSP) is fully funded by the Korean Government. It covers full tuition, a monthly stipend of approximately KRW 1,000,000, round-trip airfare, medical insurance, a settlement allowance, and one year of Korean language training before your degree begins.

Do I need TOPIK to study in South Korea?

Korean-taught programmes generally require TOPIK Level 3 or above, and many competitive universities ask for Level 4 for humanities and social science majors. English-taught programmes instead accept IELTS (usually 6.0 to 6.5) or TOEFL (usually 80 or above).

How do I apply for the GKS scholarship?

There are two routes. Under the embassy track you apply through the Korean embassy or consulate in your home country, and under the university track you apply directly to a Korean university that participates in GKS. Applications typically open in February, but embassy deadlines vary by country, so check your local embassy early.

Can international students work part-time in South Korea?

Yes, but you need permission from the immigration office. During the semester you may work about 20 hours per week, and during official vacation periods the limit is removed. Jobs are normally restricted to approved categories and you need approval from your university and the immigration office.

What is the D-2 student visa?

The D-2 visa is issued to degree-seeking international students. You apply at a Korean embassy or consulate with your certificate of admission, valid passport, academic documents, and proof of financial capacity. After arriving you must register at the local immigration office and receive an Alien Registration Card.

Can I stay in South Korea after graduation?

Yes. Graduates can apply for a D-10 job seeker visa to stay and look for work, then switch to an E-7 work visa once employed in a relevant role. Longer-term residents may later qualify for an F-2 residency visa.

What are the intake periods for Korean universities?

Korean universities have two main intakes: the spring semester starting in March and the fall semester starting in September. Applications normally open six to nine months in advance. Language programmes often run four intakes per year in March, June, September, and December.

Which universities are the SKY universities?

SKY stands for Seoul National University, Korea University, and Yonsei University. They are regarded as the most prestigious universities in South Korea, similar to how the Ivy League is viewed in the United States.

Are there scholarships besides GKS for South Korea?

Yes. Most universities offer their own merit and international scholarships, some covering up to 100% of tuition, and many reduce tuition based on your TOPIK level. Graduate research and teaching assistantships, plus separate Korean language training scholarships, are also available.

Study in South Korea Checklist

  • Have I chosen between the embassy track and the university track for GKS?
  • Have I decided between a Korean-taught and an English-taught programme?
  • Have I registered for TOPIK and/or IELTS/TOEFL?
  • Have I checked each university's deadline for the March or September intake?
  • Have I prepared my personal statement, study plan, and transcripts?
  • Have I secured two to three recommendation letters?
  • Have I applied for GKS and at least two university scholarships?
  • Have I arranged proof of financial capacity for the D-2 visa?
  • Have I applied for a dormitory or other accommodation?
  • Have I planned my immigration registration (ARC) for after arrival?
