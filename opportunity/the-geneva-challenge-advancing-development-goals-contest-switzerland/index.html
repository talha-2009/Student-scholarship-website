What Is the Geneva Challenge?

The Geneva Challenge is an annual international case competition organised by the Geneva Graduate Institute (formerly the Graduate Institute of International and Development Studies) in Geneva, Switzerland. It challenges teams of master’s students to produce innovative, interdisciplinary policy proposals that address pressing global development issues.

What sets the Geneva Challenge apart from other student competitions is its insistence on interdisciplinarity. Each team must include members from at least two different academic disciplines. This requirement is not a formality. The organisers believe that the most effective solutions to complex development problems emerge when perspectives from different fields are brought together. A team composed entirely of economics students would not be eligible, but a team combining economics, public health, and data science would be.

The competition selects one winning team from each of five continental regions. The five finalist teams are invited to Geneva, with all travel and accommodation costs covered, to present their proposals before an international jury. The top team receives the grand prize, and all five finalists receive cash awards.

About the Geneva Graduate Institute

The Geneva Graduate Institute is one of the world’s leading institutions for the study of international affairs and development. Founded in 1927, it has educated diplomats, policymakers, and scholars from over 100 countries. The institute is located in the heart of international Geneva, surrounded by United Nations agencies, international organisations, and NGOs.

The institute’s interdisciplinary approach to teaching and research makes it the natural home for the Geneva Challenge. Its faculty includes experts in international law, economics, political science, anthropology, and development studies, and its alumni network spans ministries, multilateral organisations, and civil society worldwide.

The Geneva Challenge embodies the institute’s commitment to bridging academic research with real-world policy impact. Each year’s competition theme is chosen to reflect urgent global challenges, and winning proposals are shared with policymakers and practitioners in the relevant field.

2026 Theme: Challenges to the Future of Work

The 2026 edition of the Geneva Challenge focuses on “Challenges to the Future of Work.” This theme invites participants to analyse how technological, demographic, economic, and environmental transformations are reshaping labour markets, employment relationships, and the nature of work itself across different global contexts.

The organisers encourage proposals that address specific challenges within this broad theme. Possible angles include the impact of artificial intelligence and automation on employment in developing economies, the rise of platform work and the gig economy and its implications for labour rights, the integration of displaced populations into host-country labour markets, the effects of climate change on occupational structures and livelihoods, and policies to address youth unemployment, informal employment, or the care economy.

Proposals should not simply describe a problem. They should offer an original, actionable policy intervention that accounts for the specific institutional, economic, and social context of the country or region being studied. The strongest proposals typically combine rigorous analysis with a clear theory of change and concrete recommendations.

Submission Guidelines

The Geneva Challenge requires each team to submit a written proposal of up to 10,000 words excluding references and appendices. The proposal must be written in English and submitted through the official competition portal.

The proposal should include the following sections: a clear problem statement situated within the annual theme, a review of relevant literature and existing policy approaches, a detailed description of the proposed policy intervention, an implementation plan that accounts for institutional context and resource constraints, and a conclusion that summarises the expected impact and policy relevance.

All sources must be properly cited. The appendices may include additional data, maps, charts, or other supporting material but should not exceed 15 pages. Proposals that exceed the word limit or deviate from the required structure may be disqualified.

Evaluation Criteria

Proposals are evaluated by a panel of judges drawn from academia, international organisations, and policy practice. The evaluation criteria are designed to assess both the intellectual quality of the proposal and its practical relevance.

  1. Relevance to the theme. The proposal must clearly address the annual theme and demonstrate why the chosen problem matters in the context of global development.
  2. Interdisciplinary integration. Judges look for genuine integration of perspectives from different disciplines, not superficial mentions. The proposal should show how insights from each field strengthen the overall analysis and recommendations.
  3. Originality and innovation. Proposals should offer fresh ideas or novel combinations of existing approaches. A proposal that merely summarises existing policy without adding new thinking will not rank highly.
  4. Analytical rigour. Arguments should be supported by evidence, data, and sound reasoning. Assumptions must be stated clearly, and limitations acknowledged.
  5. Feasibility and implementation. The proposed intervention should be realistic given the institutional, political, and economic context of the target country or region. Judges assess whether the proposal includes a credible implementation pathway.
  6. Clarity and presentation. The written proposal should be well-structured, clearly written, and free of errors. The oral presentation in Geneva is evaluated separately with similar criteria.

How to Apply Step by Step

Step 1: Build Your Team

Find three to five fellow master’s students who bring different disciplinary perspectives. The most competitive teams combine analytical rigour, domain expertise, and creative problem-solving. At least two distinct academic disciplines must be represented.

Step 2: Understand the Theme

Read the theme briefing on the official Geneva Challenge website. Study how previous winning proposals approached their topics. The organisers provide a theme document that frames the key questions and suggests potential angles.

Step 3: Develop Your Proposal

Choose a specific problem within the theme, conduct background research, and develop your policy intervention. Use the interdisciplinary composition of your team to approach the problem from multiple angles. Draft each section of the proposal and iterate based on team feedback.

Step 4: Register and Submit

Register your team on the official competition portal. The team leader submits the proposal and proof of enrollment for all members before the deadline. Late submissions are not accepted.

Step 5: Wait for Finalist Selection

The judging panel reviews all eligible submissions and selects one finalist team per continental region. Finalists are notified by email and invited to prepare an oral presentation for the final event in Geneva.

Step 6: Present in Geneva

If selected as a finalist, travel to Geneva with all expenses covered. Present your proposal before the jury, answer questions, and attend the awards ceremony. Even if you do not win the grand prize, reaching the final stage is a significant professional achievement.

Tips for a Winning Proposal

  • Take interdisciplinarity seriously. Do not just list team members from different departments. Show how each discipline shaped the proposal. A strong proposal will demonstrate, for example, how economic modelling informed the policy design, how legal analysis identified regulatory constraints, and how ethnographic insights ensured cultural appropriateness.
  • Choose a focused problem. The most successful proposals do not try to solve everything. They identify a specific, well-defined problem within the theme and develop a targeted intervention. A proposal about the future of work in one sector of one country is more likely to succeed than a broad proposal about global labour market trends.
  • Ground your proposal in evidence. Use credible data sources, peer-reviewed research, and official reports to support your problem statement and proposed solution. Judges are academics and practitioners who value rigorous evidence.
  • Think about implementation. A beautiful policy idea that cannot be implemented will not win. Address questions of funding, institutional capacity, political will, and timeline in your proposal.
  • Write clearly and concisely. Avoid jargon. Structure your proposal with clear headings and logical flow. Have team members who did not write a given section review it for clarity.
  • Proofread rigorously. Spelling and grammar errors undermine the credibility of your proposal. Use peer review and editing tools before submission.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Ignoring the interdisciplinary requirement. Teams that meet the letter but not the spirit of this requirement will not score well. Every section of your proposal should reflect input from multiple disciplines.
  • Choosing a topic that is too broad. The future of work is a vast subject. A proposal that tries to cover automation, informal employment, the gig economy, and child labour in 10,000 words will be superficial across all of them.
  • Submitting a purely descriptive proposal. Describing a problem is not enough. The Geneva Challenge rewards proposals that offer a clear, actionable policy intervention. Your proposal should answer the question: what should someone do differently as a result of your work?
  • Neglecting the local context. A policy that works in Switzerland may not work in Senegal. Demonstrate awareness of the institutional, cultural, and economic realities of the context your proposal addresses.
  • Waiting until the last minute. Developing a strong interdisciplinary proposal takes weeks of collaboration. Start early, establish clear分工, and build in time for revisions.
  • Forgetting proof of enrollment. Every team member must provide proof of enrollment at the time of submission. Collect these documents early to avoid last-minute scrambling.

Questions and Answers

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Geneva Challenge?

The Geneva Challenge is an annual case competition organised by the Geneva Graduate Institute. Master’s students from around the world form interdisciplinary teams to develop policy proposals addressing a global development theme. Winners receive cash prizes and an all-expenses-paid trip to present in Geneva.

Who can participate in the Geneva Challenge?

Teams of 3-5 currently enrolled master’s degree students from any university worldwide. Team members must represent at least two different academic disciplines.

What is the prize for winning?

The grand prize winner receives CHF 10,000. Four continental winners each receive CHF 2,500. All five finalist teams receive fully funded travel and accommodation to present in Geneva.

What is the theme for Geneva Challenge 2026?

The theme for 2026 is “Challenges to the Future of Work.” Proposals should address how technological, demographic, economic, and environmental changes are transforming labour markets and employment worldwide.

Can undergraduate students apply?

The competition is specifically for master’s degree students. Undergraduate students and doctoral candidates are not eligible. Each team member must provide proof of master’s-level enrollment.

Do I need to be from a specific country?

No. The Geneva Challenge is open to students of all nationalities studying at any accredited university worldwide. One finalist team is selected from each of five continental regions.

How long is the proposal?

Proposals must not exceed 10,000 words excluding references and appendices. Appendices should not exceed 15 pages. All proposals must be in English.

Can I participate alone?

No. Teams must have at least three members and no more than five. The interdisciplinary requirement also means you need at least one teammate from a different academic discipline.

What happens at the final event in Geneva?

Finalist teams present their proposals before an international jury, participate in Q&A sessions, attend networking events and lectures at the Geneva Graduate Institute, and the winners are announced at an awards ceremony.

How are proposals evaluated?

Proposals are judged on relevance to the theme, interdisciplinary integration, originality, analytical rigour, feasibility, and clarity of presentation. Both the written proposal and the oral presentation are evaluated.
